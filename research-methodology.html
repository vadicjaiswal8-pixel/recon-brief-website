The Recon Brief Evidence Review Framework

A structured approach to evaluating research evidence before drawing conclusions.

Research is useful only when its conclusions are supported by evidence that readers can examine. The Recon Brief Evidence Review Framework describes five principles for examining source quality, tracing claims to supporting evidence, checking independent corroboration, communicating uncertainty, and connecting findings to the original research question.

!

This framework is a research-quality methodology, not a guarantee that every conclusion is correct.

The evidence review framework

Five principles for
examining evidence.

Use these principles as complementary questions when reviewing a report. They are prompts for careful judgement, not a scoring system.

01 / PRINCIPLE

Source Quality

A source should be appropriate for the claim it is being used to support. Review who produced it, its purpose, the evidence behind it, its relevance, and how current it is.

Questions to consider

  • Who published the information, and what is their basis for making the claim?
  • Is the underlying evidence or methodology available?
  • Is the source relevant to the geography, industry, and time period being examined?
  • Is this primary evidence or commentary based on another source?
Warning sign

A precise statistic is repeated without a traceable link to its original source.

02 / PRINCIPLE

Evidence Traceability

Readers should be able to follow an important conclusion back to identifiable supporting evidence. Distinguish what a source explicitly states from the researcher's interpretation.

Questions to consider

  • Can the original source be located?
  • Does the source actually support the claim attributed to it?
  • Have important numbers, dates, qualifications, or context been changed?
  • Can a reader independently inspect the supporting material?
Warning sign

A source discusses the general topic but does not substantiate the specific claim being made.

03 / PRINCIPLE

Independent Corroboration

Multiple publications do not necessarily represent multiple independent confirmations. Several reports may repeat the same original statistic or rely on the same underlying study.

Questions to consider

  • Do the sources rely on independent evidence?
  • Are multiple publications repeating a single original claim?
  • Where sources agree or disagree, what explains the difference?
  • Are alternative explanations worth examining?
Warning sign

Several articles repeating one original statistic are treated as several independent confirmations.

04 / PRINCIPLE

Uncertainty and Contradictions

Good research makes material uncertainty visible. Missing information, conflicting estimates, assumptions, and limitations should not be concealed to make a conclusion appear more certain.

Questions to consider

  • Do credible sources provide conflicting estimates?
  • Are they using different definitions, time periods, or methodologies?
  • What information is missing?
  • Which assumptions could materially change the conclusion?
Warning sign

A precise forecast is presented as certain despite conflicting evidence or significant assumptions.

05 / PRINCIPLE

Decision Relevance

Research should answer the original question, and its recommendations should remain within what the evidence supports.

Questions to consider

  • Do the findings answer the original research question?
  • Are facts, assumptions, and interpretations clearly distinguished?
  • Does the recommendation follow from the evidence presented?
  • Is additional company-specific, customer-specific, or market-specific evidence needed?
Warning sign

Industry growth alone is used to claim that a particular company will succeed without examining evidence specific to that company.
