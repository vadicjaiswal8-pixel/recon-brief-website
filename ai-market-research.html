MARKET / 01
The work behind the question

Why market research
can be hard to use.

The challenge is rarely a shortage of information. It is deciding which information fits the question, how much weight it deserves, and what the evidence can reasonably support.

01 / SCATTERED SOURCES

Find the thread across sources

Industry publications, company pages, public data, interviews, and internal notes may each answer only part of a question. Researchers need a clear record of where a finding came from and what remains to be checked.

02 / UNEVEN EVIDENCE

Separate a signal from a claim

A headline, forecast, survey response, and observed transaction are not interchangeable. Without context about date, method, and coverage, a confident statement can carry more certainty than its source supports.

03 / COMPARISON

Make unlike findings comparable

Sources may define a category, customer, or geography differently. A useful report surfaces those differences instead of blending them into a single number or losing the business decision inside a long summary.

Questions worth investigating

Make the market question
specific enough to test.

Market research is most useful when it is attached to a real choice. These are starting questions to refine—not promises that a tool can answer each one completely.

01 / MARKET BOUNDARY

What market are we actually discussing?

Which geography, customer group, product category, and time period belong in the analysis—and which do not?

02 / INDUSTRY CHANGE

What developments could matter?

Which policy, technology, supply, channel, or customer changes deserve further investigation for this business question?

03 / CUSTOMER NEED

What evidence indicates a customer need?

What is directly observed, what is reported as opinion, and what additional research would distinguish interest from actual behaviour?

04 / COMPETITIVE CONTEXT

How are alternatives positioned?

What do public offers, product descriptions, and other available sources suggest about the options customers can compare?

05 / RISK & OPPORTUNITY

Which assumptions need a challenge?

What evidence would support the opportunity, what could weaken it, and which risks are still based on inference?

06 / REPORT SCOPE

What would decision-makers need to see?

Which sources, comparisons, limitations, and open questions should appear so a reader can assess the conclusion for themselves?

A practical project framework

Start with the decision,
not the data pile.

This decision-first framework is general research guidance. It differs from a software workflow: it helps a team decide what the investigation should cover and what would count as a useful answer.

01 / DECISION

Name the choice

Write down what decision the research may inform, who will use it, and what information could change the next step. This keeps interesting but irrelevant material from taking over the brief.

02 / BOUNDARY

Draw the market

Set the geography, customer segment, category, and time horizon. In India, for example, a national question and a question about one city may require different source coverage; make the intended scope explicit.

03 / SOURCE LEDGER

Record how you know

For each useful source, note its publisher, date, method, population or coverage, and limitations. Prefer original material where practical, and keep estimates, reported views, and observed data distinct.

04 / INTERPRETATION

Show the limits

Compare evidence on consistent terms, explain what it suggests, and state what it cannot establish. Finish with the assumptions, unresolved gaps, and next check that could change the interpretation.

!

A framework does not guarantee a sound conclusion. Source quality, research design, and the fit between evidence and question still need careful human review.

Choosing research software

What to look for in an
AI market-research tool.

When comparing AI market research software, test it against a real task and inspect the work behind the answer. The checklist below applies to any tool you are evaluating.

01

Source transparency

Can you identify where a key statement came from, open the source, and see whether its date and scope fit your question?

02

Evidence you can examine

Does the output make it possible to review support for important findings rather than asking you to accept a polished paragraph on trust?

03

Clear treatment of disagreement

When sources differ or coverage is thin, does the report preserve those tensions and uncertainties instead of forcing a false consensus?

04

Fit to your market scope

Check whether the tool can support the geography, segment, time period, and decision you actually need. A broad overview may not suit a narrow local question.

05

Useful report structure

Can the intended reader distinguish the question, method, observations, interpretation, limitations, and implications in the final market analysis report?

06

Room for human review

Can an analyst challenge assumptions, follow the evidence trail, and decide what needs independent confirmation before the findings are used?

Illustrative example / hypothetical

Should a small food brand
test a subscription offer?

The following scenario is invented to show how a market-research question can be examined. It is not a real company, market finding, citation, or Recon Brief report.

01 / QUESTION

Set the decision

A hypothetical Indian packaged-food startup is considering a limited subscription pilot in one city. The research question is whether there is enough evidence to test repeat purchasing—not whether a national opportunity can be assumed.

02 / SCOPE

Define the comparison

The researcher specifies the city, target customer, product category, offer period, and what “repeat purchase” means. These boundaries keep broad category commentary from standing in for evidence about the proposed pilot.

03 / SOURCES

Build a source plan

Possible inputs might include the business’s own customer records where appropriate, interviews or survey work it has actually conducted, public product and offer pages, and relevant official or industry material available for review.

04 / COMPARISON

Compare like with like

Record each source’s date, geography, method, and population. Treat a competitor’s advertised offer as evidence of positioning, not proof of demand; keep stated interest distinct from measured purchases.

05 / INTERPRETATION

Keep the conclusion conditional

A responsible report would not invent a result. It could set out what evidence would justify a small test, what would argue for more research, and which assumptions about repeat use or operating costs remain untested.

06 / LIMITS & REPORT

Make uncertainty visible

Interview samples may not represent a wider population; public pages may not show actual sales; and evidence from another place or period may not transfer. A useful brief records those gaps beside its findings and next questions.

Market research questions

Frequently asked
questions.

Practical answers for people planning, reviewing, or organizing a market-research project.

01What is an AI market research tool?⌄

An AI market research tool uses artificial intelligence to assist parts of an investigation, such as organizing material, summarizing sources, comparing information, or preparing a structured draft. The exact capabilities vary by product. It does not remove the need to define the market, inspect original evidence, and assess whether the material supports the business question.

02How can AI help with market research?⌄

Depending on the tool, AI can help sort a large body of information, surface themes to investigate, compare descriptions, and organize a report draft. Researchers should still choose suitable sources, check dates and definitions, look for contrary evidence, and verify consequential claims. A faster summary is not automatically a stronger research design.

03What should a market research report include?⌄

A useful report usually makes its decision question and market boundaries clear, explains the sources and approach used, presents findings with supporting evidence, distinguishes observations from interpretation, and records limitations and open questions. It should connect conclusions to the decision at hand without hiding assumptions or overstating what the research can establish.

04How should I evaluate evidence gathered during market research?⌄

Check who produced the material, when and how it was collected, which people or places it covers, and whether it directly supports the claim attached to it. Compare independent sources where possible, note when sources disagree, and distinguish reported opinions, estimates, and observed outcomes. Verify important figures and quotations in their original context.

05How does Recon Brief organize research findings?⌄

Recon Brief describes its research flow as Define, Research, Evidence, Analyze, and Report. The product is intended to keep relevant source material and evidence connected to findings in a structured output. What is available depends on the current application and workflow; inspect sources and review key claims rather than assuming every item has been independently verified.

06Can Recon Brief help create market research reports?⌄

Market research is among the uses described for Recon Brief, and the platform is designed to organize research into structured reports. Whether a particular project is a good fit depends on the sources, scope, and functionality available in the current app. Treat the resulting report as material to review, not as a guaranteed answer or substitute for original research.

07How is evidence-backed research different from a generic AI answer?⌄

Evidence-backed research makes the basis of a finding easier to inspect: the reader can review relevant sources, context, and limitations rather than relying only on fluent prose. A source link is a starting point for checking, not proof of accuracy. The researcher still has to judge relevance, credibility, independence, and whether the conclusion follows from the evidence.
